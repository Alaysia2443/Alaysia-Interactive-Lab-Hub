Part B
The Guided Light Library

Things that seemed better on paper:
Implications of the light: While doing the storyboard, I have used a cartoon image of a light which is pretty easy to put on top of a particular part of my scene.  In contrast, with a device a "light" is a fixed screen that you have to physically operate.. This might make the motion feel clunky or unnatural. Therefore, in an imaginary or pretend world the use of a light is more practical than in real life for the same scenario. 
Accuracy: It's easy to position the light on top of the book. Acting it out, you might realize it's difficult to position the phone precisely or make sure the right book is lit up as  the target. This could lead to the person not finding the correct book and having to redo the entire process wasting time and resources. 

New ideas that came up from the acting:
Alternative motion: Instead of a single light lighting up at once there could be a series of lights allowing the person to really get the "guided light" experience. This could mean the light is taking the user to the book by lighting up in a particular order so they can find the book more easily. 
Interaction beyond finding: What if the light changes color when the book is found? Or a particular book that is maybe popular or of a certain genre would be a certain color or number of lights flashing instead of just ones. This new way of acting out the discovery of the book could inspire a more complex interaction.

2. Room Activity Light Dorm

Things that seemed better on paper:
 Clarity of colors: In the storyboard you can label the storyboard to say the light's  particular purpose as a caption so users understand this more easily. For instance, this light color means a roommate is sleeping mad. In a real-life situation, you might find that your roommate or guest doesn't immediately understand what each color means. The colorful lights may be interpreted differently and it’s not as straightforward leading to signs needed to be added like a keys 
Privacy and interruption: The goal of the light is to avoid interruption, but what if a roommate ignores the light and comes in anyway? The light itself can't enforce its rule. This highlights a gap between what a device’s intended behavior is and real-world human behavior. In a pretend situation this is implied but in a real world situation it may not be and can cause confusion. 

New ideas that came up from the acting:
Differentiation for specific guests: You might realize that you want a different signal for a close friend versus an acquaintance which could be helpful.
User-initiated change: Instead of just a single, static color, the light could briefly flash a different color when a person physically approaches the door and then a different color when a person is waiting to see what the status of the room is. This makes the interaction more of a two-way conversation rather than one-way. 

3. The Time To...

Things that seemed better on paper:
 The "always-on" feeling: On paper, the light is a simple cue and color. Acting it out, you might feel a pressure from a device that's always telling you what to do and it could have an adverse effect.  In return this could create more stress than help. 
Context: The device may not recognize what exactly you are doing. It might tell you to "take a break" while you are driving, which is a dangerous and unhelpful cue. This shows the device's lack of understanding of the real-world context which can cause serious irreversible problems. 

New ideas that came up from the acting:
More subtle cues: The light could be subtle, maybe a gentle glow on a watch face. The less intrusive it is, the more useful it might become instead of an overwhelmingly bright colorful light. 
Personalized pace: The light could learn from your behavior. If you always ignore the "time to eat" light, it suggests a different action that better fits your needs.

## Part A. Plan 

To stage an interaction with your interactive device, think about:

_Setting:_ Where is this interaction happening? (e.g., a jungle, the kitchen) When is it happening?

_Players:_ Who is involved in the interaction? Who else is there? If you reflect on the design of current day interactive devices like the Amazon Alexa, it’s clear they didn’t take into account people who had roommates, or the presence of children. Think through all the people who are in the setting.

_Activity:_ What is happening between the actors?

_Goals:_ What are the goals of each player? (e.g., jumping to a tree, opening the fridge). 

The interactive device can be anything *except* a computer, a tablet computer or a smart phone, but the main way it interacts needs to be using light.

\*\***Describe your setting, players, activity and goals here.**\*\*

Storyboards are a tool for visually exploring a users interaction with a device. They are a fast and cheap method to understand user flow, and iterate on a design before attempting to build on it. Sketch seven storyboards of the interactions you are planning. **It does not need to be perfect**, but must get across the behavior of the interactive device and the other characters in the scene.
